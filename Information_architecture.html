the galactic canvas / information architecture

Two pages. One continuous fall from galaxy to planet.

A cover page that shows the Milky Way from above, then a single long scroll/zoom page that tilts the galaxy, pulls the viewer down through the Solar System and the Sun, and lands on each of the eight planets in turn.

00Sitemap

Structurally the site is only two pages. Page 2 is not eight sub-pages — it's one scroll/zoom canvas containing eleven scroll-triggered scenes. Treat each box below as a "scene," not a route.

01Page 1 — Cover

Pure establishing shot. No data, no UI chrome — the job of this page is to make someone want to scroll.

Milky Way, face-on static hero
content
  • Full-bleed top-down image of the Milky Way (galactic north pole view — spiral arms visible as a disc)
  • Site title: "The Galactic Canvas"
  • One-line premise: a journey from galaxy → solar system → Sun → planets
  • Scroll/tap cue, nothing else — resist adding stats here
interaction
  • On load: slow parallax drift on the starfield, title fades in
  • On scroll or click: camera begins to tilt the galaxy from top-down to the 3/4 angle used in Scene A — this tilt is the transition, not a hard page cut

02Page 2, Scene A — The Milky Way

The galaxy is now shown at an angle so its disc and arms read in 3D. Info panels reveal as the user scrolls through this scene, then the camera pushes toward one spiral arm.

Milky Way, angled view scroll scene
content blocks
  • What it is: a barred spiral galaxy
  • Structure: central bulge, spiral arms, surrounding halo, disk, dark matter
  • Scale stats: ~100,000–120,000 ly diameter · 100–400 billion stars · ~13.6 billion years old · disk ~1,000 ly thick
  • Where we sit: Solar System is ~26,000 ly from the galactic center, in the Orion Arm
  • Motion: orbits the galactic center at ~828,000 km/h — one "galactic year" ≈ 230 million years
  • Neighborhood: part of the Local Group, moving toward the Andromeda Galaxy
  • Center: Sagittarius A*, the supermassive black hole at the core
  • Name origin: "Milky Way" from its milky band in the night sky (Latin: Via Lactea)
interaction
  • Reveal: info panels pin and fade in one at a time as the user scrolls, layered over the rotating galaxy
  • Locator: a marker appears on the Orion Arm labelled "you are here," which becomes the zoom target
  • Exit: continued scroll pushes the camera toward that marker into Scene B

03Page 2, Scene B — The Solar System

Camera has arrived in the Orion Arm; the Sun and its orbiting bodies resolve into view.

Solar System, zoomed in scroll scene
content blocks
  • What it is: the Sun and everything gravitationally bound to it
  • Formation: nebular hypothesis — collapse of a giant molecular cloud, ~4.6 billion years ago
  • Census: 8 planets, 5 recognized dwarf planets, 300+ known moons, 1M+ asteroids over 1 km
  • Layout: inner rocky planets → asteroid belt → outer gas/ice giants → Kuiper Belt → Oort Cloud
  • Extent: ~2 light-years across to the edge of the Oort Cloud
  • Orbital plane: planets orbit in roughly the same plane (ecliptic) and direction, held by the Sun's gravity
interaction
  • Visual: schematic orbit rings fade in around the Sun as each stat resolves
  • Exit: continued scroll zooms straight into the Sun at center, into Scene C

04Page 2, Scene C — The Sun

Closest zoom of the "establishing" chain before the journey fans out into the eight planet scenes.

The Sun, close-up scroll scene
content blocks
  • Classification: G-type main-sequence star — a "yellow dwarf"
  • Scale: ~1.39 million km diameter (109× Earth) · 99.86% of the Solar System's mass
  • Temperature: ~5,500°C surface vs. ~15 million°C core
  • Composition: ~73% hydrogen, ~25% helium
  • Power source: nuclear fusion, hydrogen fusing into helium
  • Life cycle: ~4.6 billion years old, ~10 billion year lifespan, eventually becomes a red giant then a white dwarf
  • Phenomena: sunspots, solar flares, solar wind
interaction
  • Visual: surface/corona close-up, subtle flare animation
  • Branch point: as this scene ends, an orbital path selector or simple "continue" cue appears, launching the planet sequence
  • Exit: scroll pulls the camera outward and the first planet, Mercury, resolves into view

05Page 2, Scenes D–K — Planet template

Same layout repeated eight times, once per planet, in orbital order. This is the pattern to build once and populate eight times.

Planet scene — repeatable template ×8
content blocks (every planet)
  • Hero: full planet render, slow rotation
  • Quick-stat strip: distance from Sun, diameter, day length, year length, moon count
  • Overview line: what it's made of — rocky / gas giant / ice giant
  • Qualitative highlight: the one-sentence "why this planet is unique" fact
  • Deep-dive panel: atmosphere & surface temperature, notable moons, discovery date and exploring spacecraft/missions
interaction
  • Reveal: quick-stat strip appears first on arrival; deep-dive panel expands on continued scroll or a tap/click
  • Position cue: small orbital mini-map in a corner shows which planet is currently in view
  • Nav: prev/next arrows to jump directly between planets without re-scrolling the whole sequence

06Branch — The Earth–Moon System

A detour, not a stop on the main line. From the Earth scene, the viewer can choose to zoom further into the Earth–Moon pair before rejoining the main sequence at Mars. This is the deepest zoom level in the whole site — a good place to slow the pacing down and let the Moon get more than a stat strip.

Earth–Moon System, close-up optional branch
content blocks
  • Formation: the giant impact hypothesis — roughly 4.5 billion years ago a Mars-sized protoplanet, nicknamed Theia, struck the young Earth at a glancing angle; debris flung into orbit coalesced into the Moon
  • Physical profile: diameter ~3,476 km (about 27% of Earth's) · mass ~1.2% of Earth's · surface gravity ~1.62 m/s² (about 16.6% of Earth's)
  • Orbit & tidal lock: average distance ~384,400 km; tidally locked so the same face (~59% of the surface, counting libration) always points toward Earth
  • Timekeeping: ~27.3-day sidereal orbit vs. the ~29.5-day cycle of visible phases (new, crescent, quarter, gibbous, full) that people actually see from Earth
  • Surface & environment: no atmosphere; heavily cratered highlands alongside dark basaltic "maria" (ancient lava plains); temperatures swing from ~130°C in direct sunlight to ~-180°C in shadow; permanently shadowed polar craters are known to hold water ice
  • Effect on Earth: its gravity drives ocean tides and helps stabilize Earth's axial tilt, keeping our seasons regular over geologic time; the same geometry produces solar and lunar eclipses
  • Slow drift: tidal forces are pushing the Moon away from Earth by about 3.8 cm per year
  • Exploration: 6 crewed Apollo landings (1969–1972) put 12 people on the surface; current-era missions (Artemis, Chang'e, Chandrayaan, among others) are targeting a return, with particular interest in the ice-bearing south pole
interaction
  • Branch trigger: a distinct "zoom into the Moon" prompt on the Earth scene — visually separate from the prev/next planet arrows, so it reads as a detour rather than the next stop
  • On entry: camera pulls in on the Earth–Moon pair together, ideally rendered at true relative size and distance before easing into the Moon close-up
  • Exit: a clear "back to Earth" control returns the viewer to the Earth scene, which then continues normally on to Mars — the branch should never be the only way forward

Notes carried over from the research doc

  • Treat Page 2 as one continuous canvas built with a scroll-triggered zoom library — the research already flags Three.js for the 3D scenes and GSAP ScrollTrigger for pinning/reveal timing.
  • Moon counts for Jupiter and Saturn move as new moons get confirmed — worth a quick recheck close to launch.
  • Keep each planet's deep-dive panel collapsed by default; the quick-stat strip alone should be enough for someone who's only skimming.
  • The Earth–Moon branch pattern above (optional detour, dashed line in the sitemap) is reusable — worth considering for other stand-out moons like Jupiter's Europa or Saturn's Titan if the project has room.
  • The dwarf planets and Kuiper Belt/Oort Cloud are still only mentioned as context in Scene B and don't have their own scenes — flag if you want those added too, likely as a similar detour off the Neptune scene.
